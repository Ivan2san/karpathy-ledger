## Summary
Karpathy argues that LLMs invert the usual diffusion pattern for transformative technology. Electricity, cryptography, the internet, and GPS all flowed from governments and large institutions down to individuals; LLMs reached ordinary people first and benefit them most. The asymmetry is structural: organisations already concentrate expertise and are slowed by legacy systems, compliance, and inertia, while individuals gain "quasi-expert knowledge" in domains where they previously had none. He frames the inversion as a narrow historical window rather than a settled state.

## Key claims
- Technology normally diffuses from military and government, to corporations, to individuals. LLMs reversed the order (P100).
- Generalists gain more than specialists, because the gain lands in domains where you had no expertise to begin with (P100).
- Institutions cannot capture the same benefit: their core operations already concentrate expertise, and adoption is slowed by integration cost, legacy systems, compliance, and inertia (P100).
- The inversion is contingent, not permanent. It holds only while frontier performance stays cheap and roughly undifferentiated (P100).
- Distillation currently works against tiering by keeping capable small models available. Train-time and test-time scaling, and model ensembles, push the other way by tying performance to spend (P100).
- If money buys materially better models, large organisations reconcentrate the advantage and an elite splits from the rest, restoring the old top-down pattern (P100, and an open question).
